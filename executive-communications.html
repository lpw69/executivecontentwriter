What executive communications covers

Executive communications is the discipline of what a leader says. It includes the narrative a leadership team shares, the explanation when something changes, and the public point of view that follows. It is not a synonym for posting more often.

Stakeholder communications sits inside the same discipline. Customers, staff, candidates and partners are different readers of one company. They should not receive four unrelated stories. They should receive one story, told with the detail each of them needs. That is a writing problem and a management problem. The writing cannot fix a story the leadership team has not agreed.

Inside and outside

Internal communication fails when people cannot repeat the point. External communication fails when the public line and the internal line contradict each other. The writer's job is to keep those two honest.

A founder who tells the market one story and the team another creates translation work for everyone else. People fill the gap with rumour. Executive communications starts by closing that gap, before anyone argues about a channel.

Message before volume

A message is the sentence that has to survive a retelling. A calendar is only the plan for where that sentence appears. Teams get this backwards when they plan slots first and hope a point of view turns up later.

Write the message. Then decide the few places it deserves. A leadership meeting may be one. A note to customers may be another. LinkedIn and X are often on the list for a founder, because that is where customers, candidates and peers already look. They are not a strategy by themselves. If the message is weak, a calendar will only publish the weakness on time.
